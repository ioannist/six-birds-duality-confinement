\section{Involutive object ledger}
\label{sec:involutive_ledger}

The first piece of apparatus is the object on which the confinement
argument is allowed to operate.  The framework section explained
that SDTC is a structural law over a formed layer.  The mathematical
data inside such a layer, for the present paper, are much more
concrete: a carrier of visible objects, an involution on that
carrier, a positive measure or finite weight system, and a
Hilbert-valued observable compatible with the involution.
Throughout the rest of the paper this package is denoted
\[
  \IOL=(X,J,\mu,\psi,Y,\Jiso).
\]
It is useful to read this first as ordinary analysis.  The set
\(X\) is the space whose mass we want to localize; \(J:X\to X\)
is a symmetry with \(J^2=\mathrm{id}_X\); \(\mu\) says which
objects are visible; \(Y\) is the response space in which the
objects are observed; \(\Jiso:Y\to Y\) is the corresponding
symmetry of responses; and \(\psi:X\to Y\) is the observable.

\begin{definition}[Involutive object ledger]
\label{def:duality_confinement:involutive-object-ledger}
An \emph{involutive object ledger} is a tuple
\[
  \IOL=(X,J,\mu,\psi,Y,\Jiso)
\]
consisting of the following data:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a visible carrier \(X\);
  \item an involution \(J:X\to X\), so \(J(Jx)=x\) for every
    \(x\in X\);
  \item a positive measure, or in the finite case a positive weight
    system, \(\mu\) on \(X\);
  \item a real Hilbert response space \(Y\);
  \item a linear isometric involution \(\Jiso:Y\to Y\);
  \item a measurable readout \(\psi:X\to Y\) satisfying the
    equivariance condition
    \[
      \psi(Jx)=\Jiso\psi(x)
      \qquad (x\in X).
    \]
\end{enumerate}
The fixed locus of the ledger involution is
\[
  \Fix(J)=\{x\in X: Jx=x\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The definition deliberately separates carrier-side symmetry from
response-side symmetry.  The map \(J\) acts on the objects whose
mass is being studied, while \(\Jiso\) acts on the Hilbert space in
which those objects are read.  The equivariance condition says that
observing an object after applying the carrier involution is the
same as observing first and then applying the response involution.
For \(\Jiso\), linear isometric involution means
\[
  \Jiso^2=I_Y,
  \qquad
  \langle \Jiso v,\Jiso w\rangle_Y=\langle v,w\rangle_Y
  \quad(v,w\in Y).
\]
In topological or measure-theoretic examples one normally assumes
the displayed maps are measurable, and often that \(\mu\) is
\(J\)-invariant or at least \(J\)-quasi-invariant.  The abstract
ledger definition records only the positive measured carrier and
the equivariant readout; later hypotheses, such as separation and
trace-class positivity, state the additional structure needed for a
particular confinement argument.

This convention covers both finite and analytic situations.  In a
finite ledger, \(X\) may be a finite list of roots, defects, modes,
or objects, and \(\mu(x)>0\) is simply the weight assigned to an
object that is visible to the argument.  In a continuous ledger,
\(\mu\) is an ordinary positive measure on the carrier, and the
statements are read modulo null sets where appropriate.  The phrase
``visible support'' will always mean the part of \(X\) carrying
positive \(\mu\)-mass: positive-weight points in the finite case and
non-null measurable regions in the measure-theoretic case.  This is
the only part of the carrier that the confinement theorem can see.
Objects outside the visible support may exist as elements of \(X\),
but they play no role in the measure conclusion
\(\mu(X\setminus\Fix(J))=0\).

The fixed locus \(\Fix(J)\) is the target of the confinement
conclusion.  In a topological example it is usually a closed
subspace, but no topology is needed to name it: it is simply the set
of points left unchanged by \(J\).  The ledger terminology is meant
to emphasize that \(\mu\) is not merely background measure; it is
the accounting device that tells the theorem which visible objects
remain after the obstruction has been squeezed to zero.  The
anti-invariant projection associated with \(\Jiso\), and the
positive ledger built from it, are introduced in
\Cref{sec:anti_invariant_ledger}.

A simple model is complex conjugation.  Let \(X\) be a bounded
measurable region in \(\mathbb{C}\), stable under conjugation, with
\(\mu\) the restricted planar Lebesgue measure.  Put
\[
  J(z)=\overline{z},\qquad
  Y=\mathbb{R}^2,\qquad
  \Jiso(u,v)=(u,-v),
\]
and let
\[
  \psi(z)=(\operatorname{Re}z,\operatorname{Im}z).
\]
Then
\[
  \psi(Jz)
  =
  (\operatorname{Re}z,-\operatorname{Im}z)
  =
  \Jiso\psi(z),
\]
so the readout is equivariant.  The fixed locus is the real axis
inside \(X\).  The coordinate that changes sign is the imaginary
coordinate, so mass away from the real axis is exactly the mass
visible to the sign-changing part of the readout.  This toy model
contains no deep analysis, but it displays the geometry of the
definition: a duality, a fixed locus, and an observable whose
coordinates respect the duality.

The same pattern also explains the motivating analytic example.  In
the functional-equation symmetry of the completed Riemann zeta
function, one uses the involution
\[
  J_L(s)=1-\overline{s}
\]
on the critical strip.  Its fixed locus is the critical line
\(\operatorname{Re}(s)=1/2\).  If \(X\) is specialized to the
visible multiset of nontrivial zeros, with \(\mu\) the
multiplicity-weighted measure on that multiset, then the coordinate
\(\operatorname{Re}(s)-1/2\) is the sign-changing displacement from
the fixed line.  This paper develops the abstract ledger definition
and the confinement theorem that applies to such a shape.  The
worked single-substrate validation of the Riemann-zero instance is
the subject of the sibling paper; no result from that paper is used
here.

As a statement of record, the definition above is a definition
entry rather than a theorem: there is no proof attached to the
six-tuple itself.  The formalization appendix records its faithful
Lean realization as the corresponding involutive-object-ledger
structure.  The mathematical work begins only after this ledger is
equipped with its anti-invariant projection, trace ledger, and
separation hypotheses.
